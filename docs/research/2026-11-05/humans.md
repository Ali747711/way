# Track: Human interests and the psychology of the moment Way competes for
Date written: 2026-11-05. Method: web searches only (few full-text fetches). Where a claim comes from a search snippet or a secondary source, it is marked [secondary]. Items I could not find are listed under "Not found".

## 1. Key findings

FACTS (sourced)
- Bedtime procrastination is real and costly, but causality is unproven. A 43-study meta-analysis (searched to Sept 2021; GRADE certainty "low") found moderate links to shorter sleep, poorer sleep quality, more daytime fatigue, lower self-control and evening chronotype [1]. A 2026 review of university students (18 studies, N=35,097, provisionally accepted) links it to distress, depression and anxiety symptoms [2] [secondary].
- "Revenge bedtime procrastination" is a popular label, not a separately studied construct. I found no meta-analysis of it [1][2].
- The best-evidenced fix for bedtime procrastination is an implementation-intention style plan (mental contrasting + if-then plans). Two RCTs with students (N=510 and N=250) showed more commitment and smaller gap between intended and actual bedtime, including at 3 weeks [3]. Evidence is student-only and from one research group. I found no good RCT of smartphone restriction for this problem.
- Doomscrolling research is mostly cross-sectional. Negative affect and social internet use predicted it in a 2025 student sample (N=551) [4]; a 2026 scoping review says the evidence is limited by cross-sectional designs and self-report [5]. A 2024 study reported that fast scrolling of short videos can intensify boredom [6] [press coverage only].
- AI and thinking: evidence is correlational or short-term. Kosmyna et al. (MIT Media Lab, preprint, June 2025): 54 people, 3 groups (LLM, search, no tools); LLM group had weakest EEG connectivity, lowest sense of ownership, and trouble quoting their own essays; only 18 people did the crossover session; not peer reviewed at publication [7][8]. Popular coverage overstated it; the authors warn against claims of "brain damage".
- Lee et al., CHI 2025 (Microsoft Research + CMU): 319 knowledge workers. More confidence in AI went with less critical-thinking effort; more self-confidence went with more. Effort shifts from doing to verifying and stewarding. Self-report, not a test of skill loss [9][secondary].
- Gerlich 2025 (Societies): 666 participants; frequent AI use correlated negatively with critical thinking, mediated by offloading; younger users scored lower. Author states it is correlation, not causation [10][secondary].
- Bastani et al., PNAS 2025: about 1,000 Turkish high school students. A plain GPT-4 tutor improved practice scores but, once removed, students did worse than those who never had it. A tutor with learning guardrails largely removed the harm [11][secondary]. Single school, math only.
- A 2026 experiment (Cognitive Processing) found AI improved task accuracy and reduced mental effort without a uniform effect on critical thinking [12][secondary]. Several 2026 qualitative and review papers describe "superficial engagement" but are weak evidence.
- Curiosity: Loewenstein (1994) information-gap theory says curiosity comes from perceiving a gap in what you know; it needs some prior knowledge [13][secondary]. fMRI work he co-authored found curiosity peaked when people had a good guess but were not sure [13][secondary].
- Insight: Kounios and Beeman describe insight as sudden reinterpretation, with a right-temporal gamma burst and prior internally focused attention [14]. Danek and colleagues found solutions reached with an Aha! are remembered better after up to two weeks; the advantage seems tied to correctness, certainty and pleasure rather than sudden restructuring [15].
- Retrieval practice (testing effect) is one of the most reliable learning findings: meta-analytic effects around 0.50 to 0.56 [16][secondary]. Bjork's "desirable difficulties" frame says some slowing of acquisition improves retention and transfer [16].
- Difficulty target: Wilson et al. 2019 derived about 85% accuracy as optimal for learning in models; the link to human flow is speculation [17][secondary]. Flow-performance causality is unclear [17][secondary].
- Brain training: the 2014 Stanford/Max Planck consensus (70+ signers) said marketing claims were exaggerated and broad real-world benefit unproven; 133 scientists replied that many RCTs show benefit [18]. Simons et al. 2016 (130+ studies): gains on trained tasks, no strong evidence of broad transfer [19][secondary]. FTC 2016: Lumos Labs paid $2M, with a $50M judgment suspended, for unsupported claims about school, work and delaying decline [20].
- ACTIVE trial, 20-year follow-up (2026): speed-of-processing training plus at least one booster was associated with about 25% lower dementia diagnosis (40% vs 49%); memory and reasoning arms were not significant; diagnoses came from Medicare claims; subgroup analysis, authors cautious [21][secondary].
- Crosswords: a 2022 RCT (N=107, mild cognitive impairment) found computerized crosswords beat cognitive games on the main outcome at 12 and 78 weeks, but had no untrained control [22][secondary]. A large observational study (~19,000 adults over 50) linked regular word and number puzzles to better function; cannot show cause [23][secondary].
- Interests: Wikipedia's 2025 English top pages were dominated by news, deaths, film and TV; US politics was about a quarter of the top list [24][secondary]. Spotify's 2025 top podcasts were long-form conversation, entertainment, self-improvement and crime [25][secondary]. In a Jacobs Media survey of adults 40 and under, psychology/behavioral science ranked top-3 for Gen Z (1st) and Millennials (2nd), with nature and wildlife [26][secondary]. Pew: 70% of US adults interested in health and medicine, more than any other of nine science topics (2015) [27]; space/astronomy was the top interest of about 9% (2017) [27][secondary].
- Duolingo (company-reported, not independent): DAU/MAU 34.7% in Q4 2024; over 10M users with 1+ year streaks [28]. Education app Day-30 retention benchmarks vary from about 2% to 8% across vendor blogs [29][weak].

OPINION / INFERENCE (mine)
- Way's niche is not "brain training". The honest and defensible niche is "practice at effortful, unassisted thinking, for its own sake, at the moment you would otherwise scroll."
- The AI-offloading literature supports Way's premise (effort without lookup) but is early and correlational, so Way should cite it modestly or not at all.

## 2. Detail

### 2.1 The late-night moment
What the research says drives it: low self-control and evening chronotype (strongest, moderate correlations) [1]; negative affect and social internet use for doomscrolling [4]; boredom as a common reason for phone use [6]. Variable rewards and autoplay are widely asserted as drivers, but I did not find a 2022-2026 study I could cite directly for them. Treat as inference.

What helps: the strongest direct evidence is planning (MCII, implementation intentions) rather than willpower or abstinence [3]. I did not find a head-to-head test of substitution vs abstinence for late-night phone use. Inference: substitution is plausible (Owner's own experience: leaving the device failed, chess worked for a while), but this is not demonstrated. A substitute must meet the same needs the scroll meets: low start cost, novelty, a feeling of progress, and a defined ending (Way's "Done screen" addresses the ending, which feeds are designed to avoid).

Honesty point for Way: bedtime procrastination research says the harm of late nights is lost sleep. A 20-minute set at 11pm is better than 90 minutes of scrolling, but a sleep-first framing ("do it earlier, done in 20 minutes, then sleep") is more defensible than implying night use is fine.

### 2.2 AI and cognitive offloading
Consistent theme across Kosmyna [7], Lee [9], Gerlich [10], Bastani [11]: when the tool does the thinking, people feel less ownership, do less verifying, and perform worse unassisted afterwards. Consistent limit: small samples, self-report, correlation, one task each, preprints. The most actionable finding for Way is Bastani's: guardrails matter. A tutor that withholds the answer (Way's hint-as-question ladder, answer only after commitment) is the design that avoided harm in that trial [11]. Way's design is already aligned; it should not claim the trial proves Way works.

### 2.3 Curiosity, insight, struggle
- Information gap: curiosity needs a gap AND some knowledge to close it [13]. Implication: problems should be approachable from what a general adult knows, and framed so the gap is felt before the work starts (a question, not a lecture).
- Aha memory advantage [15]: reinforces "commit to an answer first, then see the explanation." The solution is better remembered when you generated it and felt sure.
- Retrieval practice and desirable difficulty [16]: supports no-lookup and revisiting earlier ideas later. Way has no archive or streaks, but a "return" mechanic could surface a similar problem type days later (inference).
- Difficulty: ~85% success is a model result [17]; use it as a tuning target for the "hook" problem (Words, difficulty 1), not as a claim about flow.
- SDT (autonomy, competence, relatedness): standard framework; I only reached secondary summaries [30][secondary]. Inference: Way's no-nag, no-streak design supports autonomy; the hint ladder supports competence; relatedness is the weakest area (the Wordle-style "same set for everyone" is a light form of it).

### 2.4 Do puzzles help? What Way may say
Training on a task improves that task; broad transfer is not established [18][19]. The FTC action shows the legal and reputational risk of claiming otherwise [20]. Positive but limited signals exist: ACTIVE subgroup [21], crossword RCT [22]. All are older-adult or clinical populations; Way's audience is general adults. 

Honest claims Way can make: "Practice thinking without lookup", "You'll get better at these kinds of problems", "Twenty minutes instead of scrolling", "Solutions you work out yourself are better remembered" (cite Danek-type findings carefully), "Built to make you think, not to give you answers."
Claims to avoid: "makes you smarter", "boosts IQ/memory", "prevents dementia or cognitive decline", "rewires your brain", "science-backed brain training", "fixes the damage from AI", any medical/health outcome.

### 2.5 What people find fascinating
Evidence is thin and platform-skewed. Mass-attention lists (Wikipedia, podcasts) are dominated by news, entertainment and celebrity, which is not a guide to what makes a good puzzle [24][25]. Survey data suggest durable adult interest in health and medicine, psychology/behavior (especially under 40), nature/wildlife, and space [26][27]. Inference: for a puzzle product, topic appeal matters less than framing; people engage with problems about people (psychology, money, decisions), things (how it works), and language. Not found: age-split curiosity data for money, true crime, history, languages; YouTube category data; Google Trends data.

### 2.6 Why adults return to learning apps and why they quit
Public retention data are limited to company claims (Duolingo) and low-quality vendor benchmarks [28][29]. Duolingo's streak data show streaks sustain use for many people, but this is company-reported and conflicts with Way's anti-streak principle; I did not find independent evidence on whether streaks harm or help well-being. Education apps lose most users in the first week or month [29][weak]. Identity ("I am someone who thinks") as a retention driver: not found as sourced research for apps; treat as a hypothesis. Scarcity (once-a-day) is widely credited for Wordle's appeal, but sources are commentary, not studies [31][secondary].

## 3. Implications for Way (ranked)

1. **Add a "do it earlier" pairing, not a nag.** What: optional one-line if-then prompt at the Done screen, stored locally ("If it is 10:30pm and I pick up my phone, then I open Way, then I sleep"). Why: implementation intentions had the best evidence for bedtime procrastination [3]. Fit: user-chosen, no notification, no account; respects autonomy. Effort: S. Runtime cost: none (localStorage).
2. **Make "committed answer first, then explanation" the visible core.** What: keep and highlight; when revealing, restate the user's own attempt next to the solution. Why: Aha/self-generation memory advantage [15], testing effect [16], Bastani guardrails [11]. Fit: core principle. Effort: S. Cost: none.
3. **Adopt a strict claims policy.** What: write a short claims page and copy checklist (avoid list in 2.4). Why: Simons/consensus/FTC [18][19][20]. Fit: protects trust, no ads or paywall conflict. Effort: S. Cost: none.
4. **Stop and never ship: infinite mode, "brain score", IQ-style rating, cognitive-age, "streak" counters, or health claims.** Why: those are the Lumosity pattern [20] and the scroll pattern [6]. Fit: anti-goals. Effort: none.
5. **Tune difficulty so most players succeed with effort.** What: target roughly 75-90% solve rate with hints for the weekly problems; use local anonymous self-rating ("easy / right / hard") only on device, or use owner testing. Why: 85% model [17] (speculative link to flow); competence need in SDT [30]. Fit: coach not oracle. Effort: M. Cost: none unless collecting data (which Way will not).
6. **Design problems around a felt information gap.** What: every prompt opens with a concrete, answerable question using everyday knowledge; avoid trivia that require domain knowledge. Why: Loewenstein [13]. Fit: works for non-programmers. Effort: M (content work). Cost: none.
7. **Include insight-type problems in each area, not only procedure.** What: at least one Logic/Words item per set that rewards reframing (riddle-like, "magic trick" style). Why: Aha and memory findings [14][15]. Fit: playful; owner's UI taste is bold and playful. Effort: M. Cost: none.
8. **Lean on topic breadth with human subjects.** What: wrap problems in psychology, money, nature, space, language and "how things work" themes, not news or celebrity. Why: durable survey interests [26][27]; mass-attention lists are news-driven so not a model [24]. Effort: M. Cost: none. Confidence: low-moderate, so keep owner judgment.
9. **Soft return loop via the area map, not streaks.** What: the existing fading tiles; add a weekly "revisit" problem similar to one from earlier in the week (retrieval spacing). Why: retrieval and spacing [16]; inference that streak guilt is avoidable. Fit: good. Effort: M. Cost: none, if content is bundled. Note: an archive conflicts with the current "no archive" decision, so flag for the owner.
10. **Offer a "share without spoilers" result line (Wordle-style) with no tracking.** What: copy-to-clipboard text such as "Way 11/05: 5/5, 2 hints". Why: relatedness and group-membership appeal of Wordle-type sharing [31][secondary]; SDT relatedness [30]. Fit: opt-in, no accounts, mild shame risk if scores shown. Effort: S. Cost: none.
11. **Optional on-page "why this works" text with careful wording.** What: explain that struggling and then checking is a well-replicated way to learn (testing effect), and that Way hides answers on purpose. Why: [16][11]. Do not cite the MIT or Gerlich studies as proof that AI harms thinking; at most say "early research suggests". Effort: S. Cost: none.
12. **Late-night comfort without addictive tricks.** What: dark, low-brightness default after local 9pm, a visible time-box ("about 20 min") and a Done screen with a "go to sleep" line. Why: sleep outcomes [1]; autoplay avoidance. Effort: S. Cost: none. Note: this does not claim to improve sleep.

## 4. Not found / open questions

- A sourced study of variable rewards or autoplay specifically driving doomscrolling.
